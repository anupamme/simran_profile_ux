## The question

Time banks run on reciprocity, but keeping track of who gave how much time, and to whom, is administrative work that falls on volunteers and coordinators. The project asked how new technologies could reduce that work, and help volunteers track and exchange their time for help from their community.

Blockchain-based tools for social good remain rare. People's digital literacy, difficult onboarding, and rigid systems that fit complex social settings poorly all get in the way. The companion project asked how to design around those socio-technical barriers.

## How time banking works

Time banking is a form of community exchange where people trade time-based services rather than money. For every hour of service someone gives, such as tutoring, caregiving or household repairs, they earn one time credit, which they can spend on help from another member. It builds social cohesion and supports underserved people through mutual aid.

## Methods

The project looked at digitally tokenising time exchanges, for example with blockchain technologies, to enable new complementary currencies and forms of exchange within local communities.

Building on Peoplehood, the companion project used empirical and applied research into the human-centred design of a decentralised app (DApp) on the Cardano platform. The DApp gives community members time-based currency for helping others locally, and supports proof of identity and skills through social validation.

## Who I worked with

Timebanking UK, with Evan Morgan, Harvey Everson and John Vines in Design Informatics at the University of Edinburgh. Both projects were funded by the Input Output Research Hub.
